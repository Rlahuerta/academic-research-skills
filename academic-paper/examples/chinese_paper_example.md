## Abstract

Taiwan is facing a severe demographic crisis, with the number of first-year college students declining from 273,000 in 2012 to 198,000 in 2024. Private universities are disproportionately affected by this trend. This study investigates how Taiwan's private universities are transforming their enrollment strategies in response to declining birth rates. A mixed-methods design was employed: Phase 1 involved content analysis of institutional development plans and recruitment materials from 42 private universities (2020-2024), while Phase 2 comprised in-depth interviews with enrollment directors at 12 institutions. The findings reveal three dominant transformation patterns: (a) differentiation strategy, emphasizing specialized programs and industry partnerships (64.3%); (b) internationalization strategy, expanding overseas student recruitment (47.6%); and (c) lifelong learning strategy, developing continuing education and executive programs (38.1%). Notably, universities that successfully navigated the enrollment decline tended to adopt a "portfolio approach" (implementing two or more strategies simultaneously) and shared two common characteristics: executive-level administrative support and data-driven decision-making. This study recommends that private universities establish systematic environmental scanning mechanisms and select strategy portfolios aligned with their institutional positioning, rather than relying on single-strategy reactive responses. Limitations include the focus on comprehensive universities (excluding universities of science and technology) and potential social desirability bias in interview data.

## II. Literature Review

### 1. Declining Birth Rates and Higher Education in Global Context

Declining birth rates are not unique to Taiwan. Japan, South Korea, and many European countries face similar challenges ([Fictional Organization], 2023). [Author L] and [Author M] (2009) noted that East Asian countries experience particularly severe impacts from declining birth rates due to rapid higher education expansion. Japan's experience shows that private universities began facing enrollment shortfalls from the 1990s, and by 2023 more than 50% of private universities were under-enrolled ([Fictional Agency], 2023). South Korea's situation is similar, with 40% of universities facing enrollment gaps in 2024 ([Fictional Institute], 2024).

Taiwan's distinctiveness lies in the extreme speed of its fertility decline — total fertility rate dropped from 1.68 in 2000 to 0.87 in 2023, among the lowest globally ([Fictional Statistical Agency], 2024). This means Taiwanese universities face not gradual adaptation but an abrupt structural shock.

### 2. Theoretical Frameworks for Private University Enrollment Strategy

From a strategic management perspective, private university enrollment strategy transformation can be understood through [Author K]'s (1985) competitive strategy theory and [Author I] and [Author J]'s (1995) educational marketing theory. [Author K]'s three generic strategies — cost leadership, differentiation, and focus — take different forms in the higher education context ([Author E], 2021).

In recent years, scholars have further proposed the concept of "strategic portfolio," arguing that universities should not select only a single strategy but should combine multiple strategies according to their specific conditions ([Author E] & [Author F], 2013). [Author A]'s (2023) research indicates that Taiwanese private universities' strategy choices are influenced by factors at three levels: macro environment (demographics, policy, economy), meso competition (regional university distribution, industry structure), and micro resources (faculty, facilities, finances, alumni networks).

### 3. Existing Research on Taiwanese Private University Enrollment Strategies

Domestic research can be categorized into three areas. First, differentiation strategy research: [Author C] and [Author D] (2022) found that successful private universities tend to develop 2-3 "flagship programs" as enrollment highlights rather than comprehensive disciplinary development. Second, internationalization strategy research: [Author K2] (2023) analyzed 15 private universities actively recruiting international students, finding that Southeast Asian student sources have become the primary growth area, but language barriers and cultural adaptation remain major challenges. Third, lifelong learning strategy research: [Author H] and [Author I2] (2022) noted that executive programs and continuing education have become important revenue sources for some private universities, but quality control mechanisms remain underdeveloped.

However, the above studies are largely single-strategy analyses, lacking a cross-strategy integrative perspective. This study attempts to fill this gap while extracting transferable lessons from successful cases.

## III. Research Methods

### 1. Research Design

This study employs a mixed-methods explanatory sequential design ([Author C] & [Author D], 2023). Phase 1 uses primarily quantitative content analysis to establish an overall picture of strategy transformation, while Phase 2 uses qualitative in-depth interviews to supplement interpretation and context. The integration point between the two phases is that Phase 1 findings guide Phase 2 interviewee selection and question design.

### 2. Phase 1: Content Analysis

**Sample**: 42 private comprehensive universities (excluding universities of science and technology and junior colleges), covering four regions: North (18), Central (10), South (10), and East (4).

**Analyzed Materials**:
1. 2020-2024 institutional development plans (public versions)
2. 2022-2024 recruitment materials (including websites, social media, admission brochures)
3. [Fictional Competent Authority] institutional information platform data (enrollment rates, international student ratios, continuing education revenue)

**Analytical Framework**: Based on the theories of [Author K] (1985) and [Author I] and [Author J] (1995), a coding framework was constructed comprising three main categories (differentiation, internationalization, lifelong learning) and 12 subcategories. Two researchers coded independently, with inter-coder reliability (Cohen's Kappa) of .82, reaching a good level.

### 3. Phase 2: In-Depth Interviews

**Interviewees**: Enrollment directors (deans of academic affairs or admissions directors) from 12 universities — 6 "successfully transformed" universities (enrollment rates maintained above 80% over the past five years) and 6 "challenged" universities (enrollment rates below 70%).

**Interview Method**: Semi-structured interviews, 60-90 minutes each, conducted via online video or in person.

**Interview Guide** (core questions):
1. What major adjustments has your university made to its enrollment strategy?
2. What was the decision-making process for these adjustments?
3. What difficulties were encountered during implementation?
4. What factors do you consider key to success/failure?

**Analysis Method**: Thematic analysis ([Author A] & [Author B], 2006) was employed, following the six-step process for data analysis.

## IV. Research Results

Content analysis results show that the 42 private universities' enrollment strategy transformation during 2020-2024 exhibited three main patterns, as shown in Table 1.

Notably, 27 universities (64.3%) simultaneously adopted two or more strategies, while only 15 (35.7%) adopted a single strategy. Nine universities (21.4%) adopted all three strategies.

### 2. Common Characteristics of Successfully Transformed Universities

Comparing universities with enrollment rates maintained above 80% against those below 70%, the differences in strategy content were less pronounced than expected (both groups had differentiation and internationalization strategies). However, two critical differences emerged at the "implementation level."

**Characteristic 1: Executive-Level Administrative Support**

In interviews, all 6 respondents from the successful group mentioned direct involvement of the president or board of trustees:

> "Strategy transformation is not just the admissions office's responsibility — it's the president's initiative. Our president personally chairs enrollment strategy meetings every month and is willing to reallocate resources from departments that can't recruit to those with potential." (U3, Dean of Academic Affairs)

In contrast, 4 respondents from the challenged group indicated limited presidential engagement with enrollment issues.

**Characteristic 2: Data-Driven Decision-Making**

Universities in the successful group generally established systematic data analysis mechanisms:

> "We conduct annual high school student preference surveys, track application trends over the past five years, and then use this data to decide which departments to expand and which to transform. It's not based on intuition — it's based on data." (U7, Admissions Director)

### 3. Detailed Analysis of Differentiation Positioning Strategy

Among differentiation strategies, "flagship programs" were the most common approach (22 of 27). Successful flagship programs shared three common characteristics: (1) deep collaboration with specific industries, (2) clear employment commitments or internship guarantees, and (3) interdisciplinary integration (e.g., AI + healthcare, design + marketing).

> "Our smart healthcare program was designed jointly with three hospitals and two technology companies. Students begin corporate internships in their third year, and 85% receive direct job offers upon graduation. That number is our best recruitment advertisement." (U1, Dean of Academic Affairs)

## V. Discussion

### 1. Interpretation of Key Findings

The most important finding of this study is the significance of the "strategy portfolio." A single strategy has limited effectiveness, but combining two or more strategies significantly improves enrollment performance. This finding echoes [Author E] and [Author F]'s (2013) strategy portfolio theory but also provides a new insight: the benefits of a strategy portfolio reach saturation at two strategies, with the third strategy offering limited marginal benefit.

One possible explanation is that resource-constrained private universities, when pursuing three strategies simultaneously, tend to suffer from divided attention and resources, reducing the execution quality of each strategy. As interviewee U5 stated: "Trying to do everything means doing nothing well. We eventually decided to focus on differentiation and internationalization as our two tracks, and dropped continuing education."

### 2. Dialogue with Existing Literature

This study's finding of "executive-level administrative support" as a critical success factor aligns with [Author G] and [Author H]'s (2002) findings in U.S. university transformation research. However, the distinctiveness of Taiwanese private universities lies in their board structures — some private universities have boards that are deeply involved in administrative decision-making, which in certain contexts may actually facilitate rather than hinder strategic transformation. [Author C] and [Author D] (2022) noted that board conservatism may limit innovation, but this study found that under survival pressure, board attitudes can rapidly shift toward supporting change.

The finding of "data-driven decision-making" aligns with [Author A]'s (2023) recommendations. However, interview data reveal a dimension rarely addressed in the literature: the "asymmetry" of data capability. Universities in the successful group typically maintain dedicated Institutional Research (IR) teams, whereas challenged universities mostly attach this function to the Office of Academic Affairs or Research and Development, lacking independent analytical capacity.

### 3. Practical Recommendations

Based on the research findings, this study offers the following recommendations:

**For University Administrators**:
1. Establish a "strategy portfolio" mindset — select 2 (not 1, nor more than 3) core strategies for deep cultivation
2. Ensure direct presidential-level involvement and cross-unit integration
3. Invest in institutional research teams and establish data-driven enrollment forecasting models

**For Education Authorities**:
1. Provide advisory resources for strategic transformation, not merely exit mechanisms
2. Establish cross-institutional data-sharing platforms to reduce individual university analysis costs
3. Relax enrollment quota controls to give successfully transformed institutions more room

### 4. Research Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, the sample focuses on comprehensive universities and does not include universities of science and technology (whose challenges and strategies may differ). Second, interview data depend on interviewee self-reports, which may be subject to social desirability bias — particularly, respondents in the "challenged" group may tend to underreport their own strategic missteps. Third, the study's time frame (2020-2024) coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, and the execution of certain strategies (such as internationalization) may have been disrupted by the pandemic, affecting the generalizability of the findings.

### 5. Future Research Directions

Future research could extend in the following directions: (1) incorporating universities of science and technology into the analysis, comparing strategic differences between comprehensive and technological universities; (2) tracking the long-term effects of strategy transformation (5-10 year longitudinal studies); (3) investigating the implementation details and effectiveness indicators of individual strategies in depth; (4) comparing the response strategies of Taiwanese, Japanese, and South Korean private universities.

---

## VI. Conclusion

Taiwan's private universities face unprecedented survival challenges. This study finds that the key to successfully responding to declining birth rates lies not in which specific strategy is chosen, but in building the execution capacity for a "portfolio strategy" — simultaneously advancing differentiation positioning and internationalization (or lifelong learning) as two primary tracks, supported by executive-level leadership and data-driven infrastructure.

More fundamentally, this study reveals an easily overlooked reality: declining birth rates are not merely an "enrollment problem" but a "strategic problem" requiring comprehensive transformation of institutional governance structures, resource allocation, and organizational culture. Relying solely on the admissions office to increase marketing efforts without changing the institution's core competitiveness will ultimately fail to withstand structural enrollment decline.

Taiwan's private universities still have a window of time — the period before 2030 is the critical transformation phase. Those institutions that complete their strategic transformation within this window will have the opportunity to find their place in a smaller but more refined higher education market.
