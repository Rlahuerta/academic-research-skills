# Academic Abstract Template

Fill out this schema for every abstract generated by the `academic-abstract` skill.

---

## Component 1: Background (1–2 sentences)

*Establish the domain significance. What is the broader research area? Why does it matter?*

**Example (Good):**
> Peer feedback is widely used in STEM education, yet its impact on quantitative problem-solving accuracy — where solutions have objectively correct answers — remains understudied.

**Example (Bad — too broad):**
> Education is important for society.

---

## Component 2: Gap (1 sentence)

*Point out a concrete research deficiency. Use a contrastive transition.*

**Example (Good):**
> However, existing studies focus almost exclusively on essay-based disciplines; few have isolated feedback structure as an independent variable in physics problem-solving.

**Example (Bad — vague):**
> More research is needed in this area.

---

## Component 3: Objective (1 sentence)

*State the paper's precise contribution in active voice.*

**Example (Good):**
> This study compares structured peer feedback (rubric-guided) against unstructured peer feedback on problem-solving accuracy in introductory physics.

**Example (Bad — passive, vague):**
> The effect of peer feedback on student performance was investigated.

---

## Component 4: Method (1–2 sentences)

*Describe the research design, sample, dataset, or experimental paradigm.*

**Example (Good):**
> We conducted a randomized controlled trial with 120 undergraduates, using pre/post Force Concept Inventory scores and blinded grading of weekly problem sets by two independent TAs (inter-rater reliability κ ≥ 0.80).

**Example (Bad — missing specifics):**
> Students were given feedback and their performance was measured.

---

## Component 5: Key Findings (2–3 sentences)

*Lay out the primary results with specific metrics. Avoid vague qualifiers.*

**Example (Good):**
> The structured feedback group showed a 18% greater improvement in accuracy scores (Cohen's d = 0.52, 95% CI [0.14, 0.90]). Mixed-effects modeling revealed that rubric-guided reviewers produced feedback with 40% more specific actionable comments.

**Example (Bad — no metrics):**
> Results showed that structured feedback was significantly better than unstructured feedback.

---

## Component 6: Implications (1–2 sentences)

*Suggest how these findings influence the broader field or practical applications. Scope claims to the evidence.*

**Example (Good):**
> These findings suggest that rubric design should be prioritized in STEM peer assessment systems, though generalizability to non-physics domains requires further study.

**Example (Bad — overclaiming):**
> This proves that structured feedback should be mandated in all educational settings worldwide.

---

## Full Example (APA 7.0 Style, 180 words)

> Peer feedback is widely used in STEM education, yet its impact on quantitative problem-solving accuracy remains understudied. Existing studies focus almost exclusively on essay-based disciplines; few have isolated feedback structure as an independent variable in physics. This study compares structured peer feedback (rubric-guided) against unstructured peer feedback on problem-solving accuracy in introductory physics. We conducted a randomized controlled trial with 120 undergraduates, using pre/post Force Concept Inventory scores and blinded grading of weekly problem sets by two independent TAs (inter-rater reliability κ ≥ 0.80). The structured feedback group showed an 18% greater improvement in accuracy scores (Cohen's d = 0.52, 95% CI [0.14, 0.90]). Mixed-effects modeling revealed that rubric-guided reviewers produced feedback with 40% more specific actionable comments. These findings suggest that rubric design should be prioritized in STEM peer assessment systems, though generalizability to non-physics domains requires further study.

---

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] All six components are present and clearly identifiable
- [ ] Word count is within venue limits
- [ ] Active voice is used throughout (no "was investigated," "is shown")
- [ ] Specific metrics replace vague qualifiers ("significantly" → exact effect sizes)
- [ ] Gap statement uses a contrastive transition ("However," "Despite")
- [ ] Implications are scoped to the evidence (no overclaiming)
